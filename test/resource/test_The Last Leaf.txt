In a little district west of Washington Square the streets have run crazy and broken themselves into small strips called “places.” These “places” make strange angles and curves. One street crosses itself a time or two. An artist once discovered a valuable possibility in this street. Suppose a collector with a bill for paint, paper and canvas should suddenly meet himself coming back from the same direction—without a cent having been paid!

So, to quaint old Greenwich Village, where there are many artists, came Sue and Johnsy. They had their studio in a small building at the top of a three-story brick house.

The two girls had become friends while studying art in a restaurant below. They found that they liked many of the same things, and soon they moved into a little apartment together.

Sue was from Maine. Johnsy was from California. They met in an artists’ colony and decided to share a studio.

That was in May. In November, a cold, unseen stranger, whom the doctors called Pneumonia, walked around the city, touching one person here and another there with his icy fingers. Over on the east side of the city he moved boldly, striking many people. But in the narrow streets around Greenwich Village he was not so powerful.

Mr. Pneumonia was not what you would call a gentleman. A young woman, whose blood had become weakened by the cold weather, was no match for him.

Johnsy became sick.

She lay in her bed, hardly moving. She looked out of the window toward the brick wall of the house next door.

A doctor came to see her. He examined her carefully and then spoke quietly with Sue outside the room.

“She has one chance in—let us say, ten,” he said. “And that chance is for her to want to live.”

“She doesn’t want to live?” asked Sue.

“She has made up her mind that she will not get better. When a patient begins to count the things around her and connect them with her life, there is trouble.”

The doctor left.

Sue went into the room and tried to make Johnsy interested in something else.

She talked about clothes and art. She joked about their neighbors. She even brought her drawing board into the room and tried to work.

But Johnsy did not answer.

She only looked out of the window.

Sue followed her eyes.

Outside was an old ivy vine growing against the brick wall of the neighboring building. The leaves were falling quickly because of the cold autumn wind.

Johnsy was counting.

“Twelve,” she whispered.

Sue looked outside.

“What are you counting?”

“Leaves,” answered Johnsy.

“Leaves? On that old ivy vine?”

“Yes. When the last one falls, I must go too.”

“Johnsy, don’t be silly,” said Sue. “What does an old ivy leaf have to do with you getting well?”

But Johnsy did not answer.

She continued counting.

“Eleven.”

A few moments later:

“Ten.”

Then:

“Nine.”

Sue tried to stop her.

“Please don’t count those leaves. Close your eyes and try to sleep.”

“I want to see the last one fall,” said Johnsy. “Then I will go too.”

“But I don’t want to sleep,” said Johnsy. “I want to watch.”

Sue sat beside her and held her hand.

“Dear Johnsy,” she said, “try to think about something else. Think about me. Think about your paintings. Think about the future.”

Johnsy shook her head.

“I have been waiting for that last leaf to fall. I knew it would fall tonight. Then I shall go away too.”

“Johnsy, listen to me,” said Sue. “The doctor told me that you have a very good chance of getting well. But you must help him. You must want to live.”

Johnsy did not reply.

She looked out of the window.

“Seven,” she whispered.

The light was fading outside. The wind became stronger. The rain began to fall.

The last few leaves of the ivy vine moved wildly in the storm.

Sue tried to distract her friend.

“I have to finish a drawing,” she said. “I will leave you for a little while. I’ll be right back.”

Johnsy nodded weakly.

Sue went into the next room and began working. But she could not concentrate. She was worried about Johnsy.

Their neighbor, Mr. Behrman, lived downstairs.

He was an old painter who had lived in the building for many years. He had always dreamed of painting a masterpiece, but he had never created one.

He earned a little money by posing for young artists.

He was a rough-looking old man with a short white beard. Although he often complained about everything, he had a kind heart.

Sue went downstairs to see him.

She found him in his small room.

She told him about Johnsy and her strange belief about the ivy leaves.

“Is she foolish?” Behrman shouted. “Does she really think she will die because some leaves fall?”

“She is very sick,” Sue said. “The illness has made her weak.”

“I will come upstairs,” said Behrman. “I want to see this girl who is waiting for a leaf to fall.”

They went upstairs together.

Johnsy was sleeping.

Behrman looked at the ivy vine through the window.

Only one leaf remained.

The old painter looked at it silently.

Then he looked at Johnsy.

“What a foolish idea,” he said softly.

Sue closed the curtain.

“Come back tomorrow,” she told him. “I have work to do tonight.”

But Behrman did not answer.

He continued looking at the last leaf.

That night, a terrible storm arrived.

The wind blew strongly through the streets.

Rain struck against the windows.

The old buildings shook under the power of the storm.

The next morning, when the first light came into the room, Johnsy asked Sue to open the curtains.

Sue was afraid.

She thought the last leaf must have fallen during the night.

Slowly, she opened the curtains.

The ivy leaf was still there.

Johnsy stared at it for a long time.

“It is still there,” she whispered.

The wind had blown all night. The rain had fallen heavily. Yet the leaf remained on the wall.

“It is the last one,” said Johnsy. “I thought it would surely fall during the night.”

Sue smiled.

“Maybe it wants to show you how foolish you were. The leaf has stayed there to prove that you were wrong.”

Johnsy continued watching the leaf.

After a while, she said:

“Something is wrong with me. I have been a bad girl. That leaf has stayed there to show me how wrong I was.”

“I want to live,” she said.

“I want to paint again.”

Sue hugged her.

“That is wonderful,” she said. “Now you must eat something.”

Later that day, the doctor came.

He examined Johnsy.

“She has improved greatly,” he told Sue. “Now she has a real chance. Take good care of her.”

Then he added:

“I must go downstairs. There is another patient in this building who is very sick.”

Sue asked who it was.

The doctor said:

“An old painter named Behrman. He has pneumonia. He has been sick for only two days, but his condition is serious.”

The next morning, Sue sat beside Johnsy and told her the truth.

“Dear Johnsy, there is something I must tell you.”

“What is it?”

“The last leaf was not real.”

Johnsy looked at her in surprise.

“What do you mean?”

“Behrman painted it on the wall during the stormy night. He went outside when the real last leaf fell. He painted a leaf in its place so that you would believe it was still there.”

Johnsy was silent.

“He painted his masterpiece that night,” Sue continued.

“Two days later, he died from pneumonia.”

Johnsy looked at the painted leaf outside the window.

The leaf was still there.

It had never moved.

It had given her hope.

It had saved her life.

And although Behrman had spent many years dreaming of painting a masterpiece, his greatest work was not created on a canvas.

It was painted on a wall, in the darkness of a stormy night, for someone who needed hope.
