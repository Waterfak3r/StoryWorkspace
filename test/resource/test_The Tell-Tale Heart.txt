TRUE! — nervous — very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad?

The disease had sharpened my senses — not destroyed — not dulled them. Above all was the sense of hearing acute. I heard all things in the heaven and in the earth. I heard many things in hell.

How, then, am I mad?

Listen! And observe how healthily — how calmly I can tell you the whole story.

It is impossible to say how first the idea entered my brain; but once conceived, it haunted me day and night.

Object there was none.

Passion there was none.

I loved the old man.

He had never wronged me.

He had never given me insult.

For his gold I had no desire.

I think it was his eye!

Yes, it was this!

One of his eyes resembled that of a vulture — a pale blue eye, with a film over it.

Whenever it fell upon me, my blood ran cold; and so by degrees — very gradually — I made up my mind to take the life of the old man, and thus rid myself of the eye forever.

Now this is the point.

You fancy me mad.

Madmen know nothing.

But you should have seen me.

You should have seen how wisely I proceeded — with what caution — with what foresight — with what dissimulation I went to work!

I was never kinder to the old man than during the whole week before I killed him.

And every night, about midnight, I turned the latch of his door and opened it — oh so gently!

And then, when I had made an opening sufficient for my head, I put in a dark lantern, all closed, closed so that no light shone out, and then I thrust in my head.

Oh, you would have laughed to see how cunningly I thrust it!

I moved it slowly — very, very slowly, so that I might not disturb the old man’s sleep.

It took me an hour to place my whole head within the opening so far that I could see him as he lay upon his bed.

Ha! Would a madman have been so wise as this?

And then, when my head was well inside the room, I opened the lantern cautiously — very cautiously — for the hinges creaked.

I opened it just enough so that a single thin ray fell upon the vulture eye.

And this I did for seven long nights — every night just at midnight — but I found the eye always closed; and so it was impossible to do the work, because it was not the old man who troubled me, but his Evil Eye.

And every morning, when the day broke, I went boldly into his chamber, spoke courageously to him, and asked how he had passed the night.

So you see he would have been a very profound old man, indeed, to suspect that every night, just at twelve, I watched him while he slept.

Upon the eighth night I was more than usually cautious in opening the door.

A watch’s minute hand moves more quickly than did mine.

Never before that night had I felt the extent of my own powers — of my wisdom.

I could scarcely contain my feelings of triumph.

To think that there I was, opening the door little by little, and he not even dreaming of my secret actions or thoughts.

I almost laughed at the idea.

Perhaps he heard me, for he moved suddenly in the bed, as if surprised.

Now you may think that I drew back — but no.

His room was as black as pitch with the thick darkness, for the shutters were close fastened through fear of robbers.

I knew that he could not see the opening of the door, and I continued pushing it steadily, steadily.

I had my head in, and was about to open the lantern, when my thumb slipped upon the metal fastening, and the old man sprang up in the bed, crying:

“Who’s there?”

I kept quite still and said nothing.

For a whole hour I did not move a muscle, and meanwhile I did not hear him lie down.

He was still sitting up in the bed, listening — just as I had done, night after night, listening to the death watches in the wall.

Presently I heard a slight groan, and I knew it was the groan of mortal terror.

It was not a groan of pain or of grief.

Oh, no!

It was the low, stifled sound that rises from the bottom of the soul when it is filled with fear.

I knew the sound well.

Many a night, just at midnight, when all the world slept, it had risen from my own heart.

I knew what the old man felt, and I pitied him, although I laughed inwardly.

I knew that he had been lying awake ever since the first slight noise, when he had turned in his bed.

His fears had been growing ever since.

He had been trying to convince himself that there was nothing wrong.

But he could not.

The darkness and the silence made his terror greater.

And now, as I slowly opened the lantern, a single thin ray of light fell directly upon his eye.

It was open — wide, wide open!

And I saw it clearly.

The pale blue eye.

The terrible eye.

My anger grew stronger.

I could hear a faint sound.

It was the sound of the old man’s heart.

The sound grew louder.

I became certain that the old man must hear it.

The sound increased.

Louder.

Louder.

I thought the old man’s terror must be extreme.

And now a new anxiety seized me.

The sound might be heard by a neighbor!

The old man’s hour had come.

With a loud cry, I threw open the lantern and rushed into the room.

He screamed once.

Only once.

In an instant, I pulled him onto the floor and overturned the heavy bed over him.

For many minutes the heart continued to beat with a muffled sound.

But I did not fear.

The sound would not be heard through the walls.

At last it stopped.

The old man was dead.

I removed the bed and examined the body.

Yes, he was completely still.

I placed my hand upon his heart and kept it there for many minutes.

There was no movement.

He was stone dead.

His eye would trouble me no more.

If still you think me mad, you will think so no longer when I describe the wise precautions I took in hiding the body.

The night was almost gone, and I worked quickly but silently.

First I separated the body into pieces.

Then I removed the boards from the floor and placed everything beneath them.

I arranged the boards carefully.

No one could have discovered anything.

There was nothing to wash away.

No mark.

No stain.

No evidence.

When I had finished, I smiled.

My work was complete.

The old man’s death was hidden perfectly.

At four o’clock, while I was still working, there came a knock at the street door.

I went down to open it with a cheerful heart.

What had I to fear?

Three officers of the police entered.

A neighbor had heard a scream during the night and had reported it.

The police had come to investigate.

I smiled, because I knew they would find nothing.

“Gentlemen,” I said, “I welcome you. The scream was my own. I had a bad dream.”

I showed them through the house.

I told them that the old man had gone away to visit friends.

I took them into his room.

I showed them his possessions, untouched and secure.

They seemed satisfied.

I was confident.

I brought chairs into the room and invited them to sit.

I placed my own chair directly above the place where the old man’s body was hidden beneath the floor.

The officers talked about ordinary things.

I answered happily.

But soon I began to feel uncomfortable.

My head started to ache.

A strange sound appeared in my ears.

At first it was faint.

A quiet beating.

Like a distant clock.

I continued talking.

But the sound became clearer.

It grew louder.

Louder.

I wondered if the officers could hear it.

No.

They were smiling and talking normally.

Could they know?

Impossible.

They were pretending not to notice.

The sound became unbearable.

The beating grew faster.

Louder.

Louder.

“Fools!” I shouted.

“Do not pretend that you do not hear it!”

“I admit the deed!”

“Take up the boards!”

“Here! Here!”

It is the beating of his hideous heart!
